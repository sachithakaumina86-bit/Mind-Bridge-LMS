Why do we choose u = x and dv = e^(2x) dx in ∫ x e^(2x) dx instead of setting u = e^(2x) and dv = x dx? Wouldn't both integrate fine?

Socratic AI Reasoner Pedagogical Mode 10:43 AM

That is a foundational question, Kavindu. In mathematics—and especially under Sri Lankan A/L Combined Maths marking rubrics—our fundamental objective in Integration by Parts is algebraic simplification, not simply finding expressions that are mechanically integrable.

PATH A: YOUR PROPOSED INVERSION cancel
Let u = e^(2x) ⇒ du = 2e^(2x) dx
Let dv = x dx ⇒ v = (1/2)x²
∫ u dv = (1/2)x² e^(2x) − ∫ x² e^(2x) dx
Result: The integrand power grew from x to x²! The integral became harder.
PATH B: ILATE CONVENTION check_circle
Let u = x (Algebraic) ⇒ du = 1 dx
Let dv = e^(2x) dx ⇒ v = (1/2)e^(2x)
∫ u dv = (1/2)x e^(2x) − ∫ (1/2)e^(2x) dx
Result: The polynomial factor vanished to a constant (1)! Elementary solution achieved.

Notice what occurred? By selecting u as the algebraic term, differentiation collapses the polynomial degree (x → 1), leaving only the exponential function in the remaining integral.
